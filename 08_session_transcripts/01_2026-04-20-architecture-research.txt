## Now: first-principles design in detail

You asked what first-principles design means. This is going to be longer because it's the substance of everything.

First principles means: forget what other products do, forget what the current code does, forget the BHK convention. Ask *what does a house actually need to do for the people living in it?* Work up from the answer.

Here's the decomposition:

**A house does six things** for the people living in it. Any good design is judged by how well it does these six things, weighted by what the particular family cares about.

**1. It shelters the right number of people in the right rooms.** Not "3 bedrooms" but "sleeping space for the two adults who want privacy, the two kids who need quiet for school, and the grandparent who can't climb stairs." This drives room count, floor placement, and attached-bathroom decisions. Bedroom count comes out of counting people and their relationships, not from a BHK label.

**2. It supports how they actually live day-to-day.** If the family cooks daily Indian food, the kitchen needs to be ≥80 sqft, have a utility for dirty dishes and washing machine, a store for provisions, good ventilation because of tadka smoke, and a service yard. If they eat out, the kitchen can be 60 sqft with just a microwave and a small fridge. Same plot, same city, same family size — totally different kitchen. The engine should ask this, not assume it. Same logic for: how often they host guests (drives dining room vs extended living room), whether they work from home (drives home office), whether they have a daily pooja (drives pooja room sizing and placement, including NE orientation for strict Vastu), whether they have a maid who stays (drives servant quarter + servant bath).

**3. It flows well for the daily routines.** This is *circulation*. Morning: someone goes from master bedroom → bathroom → kitchen → dining → front door. Evening: kids come in from front door → wash up in guest bath → living room. Night: from bedrooms to bathrooms without crossing the living room where guests might be sitting. A good engine simulates these paths and checks they don't intersect awkwardly. No bedroom door opens directly into the living room without a buffer. No one walks through the kitchen to reach a bathroom. Morning paths and evening paths don't collide. The old `buildease_arch_intel.py` had the right idea here but stubbed it out.

**4. It gives each space the right kind of natural input.** Every habitable room needs light and air — NBC requires it, but good design exceeds the minimum. Kitchens want east or north-east light (morning sun for cooking, cooler afternoon). Master bedroom wants south or southwest in cold climates (winter warmth), north or northeast in hot climates like Bangalore (no afternoon heat). Pooja room traditionally wants NE. Living room wants the view, usually the street side. Bathrooms need exhaust — either an external wall with window or a shaft. The solar engine + ventilation engine in `buildease_arch_intel.py` pointed at this but didn't deliver.

**5. It respects privacy gradients.** There's a social pattern in every house: visitors first see the foyer or living room (public), then maybe the dining (semi-public), and never the bedrooms (private) or the utility/servant area (service). A good layout makes this gradient automatic — a stranger entering the front door should not be able to see into a bedroom. The master suite should be furthest from the entry. This is encoded as "privacy distance" — graph-distance from front door to each room. The old engine had `PrivacyZoning` stubbed; it should be one of the scored dimensions.

**6. It can actually be built, affordably, to last.** Walls on structural grid (columns at 12–15 ft centres). Bathrooms stacked floor-over-floor for single plumbing stacks. Kitchens stacked or at least on the same wall as the bathrooms above for shared wet-wall plumbing. Staircase directly above itself on each floor. No cantilever more than 4–5 ft without engineering review. Minimum window sizes per room type. Minimum door widths per room type. Every one of these has a cost consequence — if you violate stacking, plumbing cost doubles. This is where the cost engine and the layout engine have to talk to each other.

**How this translates into engine behaviour:**

The engine takes the user's intent profile — their answers to *who lives here, how do you live, what's non-negotiable, what matters most* — and translates each answer into a set of *soft constraints* with weights.

"I cook daily" → kitchen area weight goes from 0.3 default to 0.7. Store room becomes required.
"My parents visit 4 months a year" → guest bedroom required, weight 0.8. Preferably on GF. With attached bath.
"I work from home" → home_office required, weight 0.9. On a floor where I'm not disturbed by kids. External wall for light.

## Patterns across these problems — so we can avoid them by design

Looking at all 15 together, four patterns emerge:

**Pattern A — Fix-as-bandage.** When a problem appeared, previous sessions added a check or a special case rather than redesigning. Rear gap → add rear_terrace. Bathroom transit → add a detector. ## On user preference vs architectural reasonableness

You said it exactly right: **"user preference first but practical, not unreasonable — if unreasonable, you explain through the design and architecturally show it, and there should be understanding between you two."**

This is how a good architect works with a client. The client says "I want the pooja room facing west." A bad architect says "no, pooja must be NE" and overrides. Another bad architect says "ok" and builds a pooja room that gets harsh afternoon sun, no morning prayer light, culturally wrong direction. A good architect says: *"We can put it west if you want. Here's what happens — afternoon sun through the deity, no morning light during prayer time, and traditionally this direction is considered unfavorable. Most families who want the pooja room away from the NE choose north-east-north instead, which keeps the morning light. Want me to show you both and you decide?"*

That's the interaction we're building. Code-wise it means:

**User request → engine attempts → if it violates a soft preference, engine does it anyway and flags it** with a human-readable explanation and an alternative. *"You asked for the kitchen on the west wall. We've placed it there. Two things to know: (1) west wall gets harsh afternoon heat, which makes cooking hot in summer and increases food spoilage. (2) The exhaust will blow into your neighbour's east wall — their objection risk. We could move it to the east for ₹0 extra cost. Want to see both?"*

**User request → engine attempts → if it violates a hard rule (NBC minimum, structural impossibility, unsafe), engine refuses with explanation.** *"You asked for a 6ft-wide master bedroom. NBC requires minimum 8ft for habitable rooms, and structurally anything below 10ft can't fit a queen bed. Do you want an 8ft bedroom or a 6ft study room? Your choice changes what the room can be used for."*

Three categories of rules, made explicit to the engine and the user:

1. **Hard rules (engine refuses, shows why):** NBC minimums, structural impossibilities, fire safety, no-go adjacencies like WC-next-to-kitchen
2. **Strong preferences (engine obeys user but warns):** Vastu directions, solar orientation, circulation depth, traditional Indian practices
3. **Soft preferences (engine quietly optimises):** natural light ranking, storage placement, wet-wall stacking for cost

The UI reflects this. When the engine shows a layout, it says *"here's what we did and here are the 2–3 decisions where your preference competed with architectural best practice — we followed your preference and here's the trade-off."* Never silent. Never overriding.

This is the relationship between user and engine you're describing. And it's what makes your tool honest — same honesty as the cost breakdown. No hidden overrides, no hidden margins.

## On building a construction-architecture LLM (for later)

You asked how — I'll keep this brief since it's a side project for after launch.

A domain-specific LLM for Indian construction architecture is genuinely buildable. Here's the real answer:

**You don't train from scratch.** Training a large language model from zero costs ₹50–500 crore depending on size. Nobody sane does that. What you do is **fine-tune an existing open model** — Llama 3, Mistral, or their descendants — on your domain corpus.

**The domain corpus is what takes 6–12 months to build.** You'd need:

- Every Indian Standard code digitised (IS 456, IS 800, IS 875, IS 1893, IS 962, IS 11268, IS 16700, NBC 2016 all parts, ECBC)
- Every state PWD SOR for the past 5 years
- 500+ annotated floor plans with captions ("this is a 30×40 north-facing Bangalore bungalow, the master bedroom is in the SW because...")
- 1,000+ BOQ examples with real cost breakdowns
- The textbook corpus — Neufert, Ching, Punmia, Varghese, Shah, Murthy, Fleming, etc. (legally — you'd buy rights or partner)
- Real architect interviews transcribed ("why did you place the staircase here?")

**What the corpus looks like, structured for later LLM fine-tuning:**

Three data types, captured as we build:

**Type 1 — Instruction-response pairs for architectural reasoning.** For every design decision we make during development: `{"instruction": "On a 30×40 north-facing Bangalore plot with 3 bedrooms, where should the pooja room go and why?", "response": "NE corner adjacent to living room. NE gets morning prayer light through east wall. Adjacency to living room keeps the sacred space in the public zone without routing through service. Avoid placement next to guest WC (hygiene) or kitchen (cooking smells reach deity)."}`. These come directly from our sessions. If we log them properly we get 5–15 of these per conversation, possibly 500+ by the time the engine is done.

**Type 2 — Labeled floor plans.** For every plot the engine successfully designs (eventually thousands), we save the input brief, the output placement, and a natural-language description of why the design made its key decisions. This becomes the visual-reasoning dataset.

**Type 3 — Code-to-rule pairs.** Each rule we encode in `room_rules.py`, `topology_selector.py`, etc., gets annotated with its natural-language equivalent — "kitchen prefers east wall because of morning light and exhaust ventilation" — linked to the source (Neufert page 40, NBC Part 4 section 4.5.1, etc.). This is the structured-knowledge dataset.
